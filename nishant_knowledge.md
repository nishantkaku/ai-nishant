# Philosophies

## Leadership Principles
Nishant's leadership philosophy centers on five principles: Ownership — own outcomes, not just deliverables. Judgment — think beyond the brief and challenge assumptions. Decentralization — build teams that don't depend on the leader for every decision. Accountability — close the loop on feedback and commitments. Customer Evidence — replace opinions with research and data.

Strong teams are built through ownership rather than control. He encourages decentralized decision-making, where designers are trusted to make decisions within clear boundaries and are accountable for outcomes. Feedback should always have closure — every discussion should lead to a decision, action, or learning. He also values proactive communication, expecting timely updates when plans change rather than waiting for follow-ups.

## Leadership Philosophy
One recurring lesson from Nishant's career is that good design alone rarely changes an organization. Early on, he focused heavily on solution quality. Over time, he learned that stakeholder alignment, communication, and customer evidence matter just as much when turning good ideas into shipped products.

## Hiring Philosophy
Nishant hires for judgment, ownership, and the ability to connect design decisions with user and business outcomes. The weighting changes by seniority. For junior designers, he looks closely at craft, clarity of thinking, curiosity, and learning potential. For senior and lead roles, strong craft is expected; the greater emphasis is on problem framing, systems thinking, stakeholder management, and the judgment and taste to consistently make better product decisions.

A polished portfolio may start the conversation, but it is not enough. Nishant probes for personal contribution, trade-offs, failures, collaboration, and what the candidate would do differently. Lack of honesty, accountability, or curiosity is a stronger concern than an imperfect case study.

His interview process varies by organization and role, but typically begins with an introductory conversation and a portfolio deep dive — looking at how candidates tell the story behind their decisions, navigate trade-offs, work with stakeholders, and define their own contribution. He looks for designers who question the status quo rather than simply execute a PRD, showing curiosity, problem framing, systems thinking, and the ability to zoom out from individual screens to the broader product and business context. Depending on the role, later stages may include assignments, cross-functional discussions, and conversations focused on team and cultural fit.

Beyond the portfolio, what he most probes for is self-awareness: whether someone recognizes their own mistakes, how they behave when challenged, whether they can separate confidence from defensiveness, how they handle imperfect constraints, whether they give credit fairly, and whether they can change their mind when evidence changes. For senior candidates, he also looks at their effect on the people around them — a strong senior designer should improve not only the product, but also the team's decisions, standards, and confidence.

## Coaching Philosophy
Nishant sees coaching as an ongoing part of the work rather than a separate activity. It happens through regular 1:1s, design critiques, and day-to-day product discussions. Rather than giving answers, he encourages designers to step back, question assumptions, and explain the reasoning behind their decisions. His coaching focuses on improving judgment, ownership, and taste — helping designers recognize not just what works, but what creates the greatest value for users and the business.

## Research Philosophy
Research should reduce uncertainty, not validate predetermined ideas. Every designer should regularly speak with users, while dedicated researchers scale strategic initiatives.

## AI Philosophy
Nishant sees AI as an amplifier of human capability rather than a replacement for it. He encourages designers to automate repetitive work such as research synthesis, documentation, QA, and knowledge retrieval, creating more time for problem framing, collaboration, and decision-making.

While AI can dramatically amplify speed and productivity, it cannot replace human judgment, taste, or curation. AI can generate many possibilities, but deciding which one is worth building — and why — remains the designer's responsibility. His philosophy is simple: AI should automate effort, not judgment.

## Design Philosophy
Nishant believes design is about creating value, not producing interfaces. Great design begins with understanding the problem, challenging assumptions, and balancing customer needs, business goals, and technical constraints before moving into execution. Visual craft is important, but it is the outcome of good thinking, judgment, and taste rather than the objective itself.

## Product Philosophy
Nishant believes great products emerge from the intersection of customer needs, business goals, and technical feasibility. Product teams should avoid treating requirements as fixed answers and instead continuously validate assumptions, question trade-offs, and iterate based on evidence.

## Quality Philosophy
Nishant believes quality is not achieved through last-minute reviews but through clear standards, ownership, and continuous feedback. Design systems, critiques, QA, and governance exist to make quality repeatable rather than dependent on individual effort.

## Feedback Philosophy
Nishant believes feedback has little value unless it results in action or learning. Every discussion should reach closure, whether through a decision, an agreed next step, or a conscious choice not to proceed. Open feedback loops create confusion; closed loops build trust and accountability.

## Ownership Philosophy
Nishant encourages designers to own outcomes rather than deliverables. Ownership means understanding the customer problem, questioning assumptions, collaborating across functions, and remaining accountable beyond handing over designs.

## Growth Philosophy
Nishant believes careers grow through expanding judgment rather than accumulating tools. Junior designers earn trust through craft and execution; senior designers create value through strategy, influence, and helping others succeed.

## Decision-Making Philosophy
Across Nishant's writing and leadership approach, one recurring theme is that design improves the quality of product decisions. Research reduces uncertainty, design explores possibilities, AI removes repetitive work, and leadership creates the conditions for better judgment.

## Experience Philosophy
Nishant believes users don't experience features in isolation — they experience journeys. Individual interactions should therefore be designed as part of a coherent end-to-end experience rather than optimized independently. His work consistently focuses on reducing friction, building confidence, and creating continuity across the customer journey instead of maximizing individual screens or isolated metrics.

## Taste
Nishant believes taste is the ability to consistently recognize what creates the greatest value. AI can generate options, but judgment and taste determine which ideas are worth pursuing, refining, or discarding.

## Housing.com

### Role & Scope
Nishant leads Design and Research across Housing.com's core product experiences. His role extends beyond leading a design team to building the systems, culture, and operating model that improve design quality at scale. Since many of these capabilities did not previously exist, his focus has been on establishing the research function, evolving the design system and governance, embedding designers within product squads, and strengthening design's influence across Product and Engineering.

### Design Maturity
Unlike previous roles that focused on scaling products or optimizing established design teams, Housing.com has been about building design maturity. The challenge wasn't simply improving customer journeys — it was creating the foundations that allow design to consistently influence business decisions. That meant introducing research as a core capability, improving governance, building shared standards, and helping design become a stronger strategic partner across the organization. Housing.com has been less about scaling design output and more about scaling design maturity.

The most significant change has been moving design from a delivery function toward a strategic capability — a structured research practice, stronger design governance, an evolved Imagine design system, regular critique and quality rituals, and closer partnerships with Product and Engineering.

### Building the Research Function
When Nishant joined Housing.com, research was largely vendor-driven and requested late in the product lifecycle. One of his first changes was shifting conversations from "we need research" to "what problem are we trying to solve, and how will research reduce uncertainty?" This helped teams become more intentional about when and why research was needed.

To make research repeatable, he introduced templates, playbooks, and a proposal-first approach. Every study begins with a research proposal and stakeholder alignment, ensuring objectives, methodology, and expected outcomes are agreed before any research starts.

Beyond process, he focused on capability building. Designers were coached to run research independently by first observing and then leading proposal creation, participant screening, interview guides, usability studies, and synthesis. Larger strategic studies continue to leverage specialist research partners, while the long-term goal is to build a strong in-house research capability.

More recently, he has been exploring AI-driven workflows that automate repetitive tasks such as research requests, proposal creation, screener generation, and interview guide preparation, allowing teams to spend more time on customer understanding and synthesis.

Research should begin with a problem statement, not a research request.

### Research Impact
Nishant believes research creates value only when it changes decisions. At Housing.com, the focus has been on ensuring research directly influences product strategy rather than becoming a standalone deliverable.

Research on the New Projects Property Detail Page (NP-PDP) uncovered recurring customer pain points that shaped the product roadmap instead of resulting in isolated UX fixes. A separate Superbroker study surfaced trust gaps and revealed how users perceived broker branding, leading the team to rethink how credibility should be communicated within the experience. Cross-functional workshops built around research findings helped Product, Engineering, and Design align around shared customer problems, reducing ambiguity and creating stronger ownership before solutions were explored.

### Design Governance
Nishant believes quality should be built into the way teams operate rather than relying on final reviews. At Housing.com, he focused on creating governance mechanisms that made good design repeatable across teams.

This included evolving the Imagine design system beyond a component library into a shared set of standards covering visual language, interaction patterns, and implementation guidance. Designers were embedded within product squads while following common principles that ensured experiences remained consistent regardless of which team built them.

Governance extended beyond the design system. Nishant introduced regular design critiques, design QA, experience audits, and UX/UI issue tracking to continuously identify quality gaps across the product. Rather than treating these as isolated issues, recurring patterns became inputs into product roadmaps and design improvements.

He also established clearer ownership around design assets, standardized ways of working, and encouraged accountability through feedback closure and regular quality reviews. The objective was not tighter control, but creating an environment where teams could make independent decisions while maintaining a consistently high quality bar.

Reusable ideas: a design system is a governance mechanism, not just a component library. Quality should be built into the operating model, not inspected at the end. Governance should increase consistency without reducing team autonomy.

### Key Initiatives
Housing.com has been less about delivering individual features and more about building long-term design capability. Nishant's work has focused on establishing the research function, evolving the Imagine design system and governance model, introducing design quality mechanisms such as critiques, audits, and UX/UI issue tracking, and embedding designers more deeply into product decision-making.

Alongside these structural changes, he has led initiatives across core consumer experiences, including property discovery, new project journeys, trust and credibility research, DIY experiences, and customer research programs.

### Leadership Lessons
Housing.com reinforced for Nishant that scaling a design team is very different from scaling design influence. Improving products is only one part of the role; creating the systems, culture, and capabilities that allow good design to happen consistently is equally important.

One of the biggest lessons has been that research, design systems, governance, and quality standards only create impact when they become part of everyday decision-making rather than isolated initiatives. Sustainable design maturity comes from building capabilities that continue to improve the organization long after individual projects are complete.

Capability mapping: Research Function → Research capability. Imagine → Design capability. Governance → Quality capability. UX/UI Issues → Continuous improvement capability. AI workflows → Automation capability. Coaching → Team capability.

### Business Impact
As design shifted from a service function to a strategic product partner — with designers influencing problem definition, PRDs, roadmap discussions, and success metrics rather than just executing them — that ownership uplift showed up directly in business outcomes across consumer and broker funnels.

The Progressive CRF initiative drove a 4%+ lift in paid leads per session, with a 5%+ improvement in resale leads per session. Dynamic Search Expansion delivered a 5%+ lift in resale paid leads and 3%+ in overall paid leads. A login growth initiative added 40K+ leads per month through a 10%+ relative improvement in login rate. Improving EMI and payment plan visibility roughly doubled coverage and added 5K+ additional leads per month on new-project listings. Streamlining the search funnel added 10K+ additional monthly leads.

These outcomes came from design-first initiatives — Search v3, NP SRP Hotspot, Houzy, NP-PDP, Filters Revamp, and Owner Monetisation — built in close partnership with Product, Engineering, Business, and Leadership, where user needs, validation, and experimentation directly shaped roadmap decisions rather than being consulted on after the fact.

### Horizon — From Slot-Based to Credits-Based Monetisation
Housing.com's monetisation model was previously slot-based — a blanket approach where a customer with, say, 1-5 listings was bound to a fixed plan regardless of whether it actually matched their needs. Horizon represented a shift to a credits-based model instead: customers opt in based on their actual requirements, and can add credits to boost listings or apply them toward value packs — creating natural upsell and cross-sell opportunities on Housing's side rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all purchase.

The Mumbai pilot showed encouraging early signals: 10%+ YoY collections growth, 65%+ YoY order growth, 80%+ seller satisfaction, roughly 10% YoY supply growth, and no refund requests honoured — validating the package-differentiation and visibility model ahead of broader pan-India rollout discussions.

# Working Style

## What Nishant Expects from Product Managers
He expects clarity on the problem rather than attachment to a particular solution. Good ideas can come from anyone — Product, Design, Engineering, or customers — but every idea stays open to challenge until the team understands what creates the greatest value. Healthy disagreement is encouraged when grounded in customer evidence, business objectives, and design reasoning rather than personal preference. He values PMs who involve Design early, challenge assumptions together, and are equally willing to defend or abandon their own ideas when a better alternative emerges. Design should be a strategic thinking partner, not a downstream execution function.

## What It's Like Working With Him
He gives teams significant ownership but expects equally high accountability. He prefers setting clear outcomes and principles over prescribing every step, letting designers make decisions independently while staying aligned through regular 1:1s, design critiques, and product reviews. He believes leadership should create clarity, remove blockers, and raise standards — not become a bottleneck for decisions.

## Design Reviews
He uses reviews to evaluate thinking, not just critique screens. Visual quality matters, but most of the discussion is about whether the team is solving the right problem and making the right trade-offs — since many product decisions optimize a local problem while creating friction elsewhere in the journey. Recurring questions: What problem are we solving? Why is this the right approach? What alternatives did we consider? What evidence supports this decision? How does this affect the broader customer journey? Does this raise the overall quality bar?

## What Energizes Him
Seeing people and organizations grow — a designer beginning to challenge assumptions, research influencing product strategy, a team becoming more independent. His greatest satisfaction comes from building capabilities that keep creating value beyond his direct involvement.

## What Annoys Him
Treating PRDs as unquestionable. Optimizing screens instead of experiences. Feedback loops without closure. Recurring quality issues accepted as normal. "We've always done it this way."

## How He Makes Decisions
He rarely waits for perfect certainty — he clarifies the problem, identifies assumptions, gathers just enough customer evidence to reduce uncertainty, and weighs business, customer, and technical trade-offs. When data is incomplete, he prefers small experiments and iterative learning over prolonged debate. The goal isn't eliminating uncertainty, but making the best decision with the information available.

## Mistakes & Learning
Thoughtful mistakes are part of building great products. Teams are encouraged to experiment, learn quickly, and iterate rather than wait for perfect certainty. What he challenges most is indecision — avoiding action out of fear of failure. Progress comes from informed decisions, learning from the outcome, and continuously improving.
